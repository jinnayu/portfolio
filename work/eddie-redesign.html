01 / The brief

The product had evolved.
The experience hadn’t.

Eddie’s existing website worked, but its presentation felt dated. For an intimate health product, that gap mattered: the experience needed to communicate quality, discretion, and trust before asking someone to buy.

The brief began as a visual refresh. My work connected that ambition to a clearer product story, better sizing guidance, and a consistent journey from campaign email to product page.

A change in how the product is introduced

PRODUCT FIRST

Make the device and its value immediately visible.

LESS COMPETING HIERARCHY

Give the main message and next action space to stand out.

CONFIDENCE BEFORE COMMITMENT

Invite customers to learn whether Eddie is right for them.

02 / Finding the direction

Looking outside
the category.

I looked beyond health-product websites to Apple’s AirPods Max page: a product experience that used scroll, staged reveals, and depth to make an unfamiliar object feel considered and worth understanding.

I brought that reference to the Head of UX and proposed a quick internal concept test. The positive response gave the team a direction to explore before committing to the full design.

How might we make an intimate health product feel premium, trustworthy, and worth understanding?

PHASE 01 · MAR 2023–APR 2024

Establish the direction.

A take-home redesign of the live PDP led to my role. Working with Katie and Harrison, I explored the product narrative and helped carry the parallax direction into two redesigns.

Team & contribution

Katie — UX/UI Designer. Harrison — Head of UX. My early work included redlining the existing PDP and developing a high-fidelity wireframe in under a week. The source notes describe two redesigns shipped within five months.

PHASE 02 · APR 2024–APR 2025

Raise the level of craft.

Following a team restructure, I continued with Eugene and DM. I owned wireframes, high-fidelity mockups, and interactive prototypes while coordinating requests across engineering and marketing.

Team & constraints

Eugene — VP of Design. DM — Art Director. The design direction carried forward, while the execution became more ambitious. Product work ran alongside paid advertising and email requests; the final design, QA, and launch sprint took 4–6 weeks.

03 / The experience strategy

A product story that answers
the next question.

Understand

What is Eddie, and why would I consider it?

Evaluate

How does it work, and what makes it different?

Find the fit

How do I size and use it correctly?

Take a step

Is it right for me, and what happens next?

Decision 01 / Product education

Show how it works.
Then help people use it.

Motion was part of the explanation. I designed staged product storytelling and supporting how-to content so the experience could move from initial interest to practical understanding.

Why this decision mattered

A premium visual direction alone would not answer a customer’s questions. Product explanation and use guidance needed a clear place in the page hierarchy, rather than relying on campaign language to do every job.

Decision 02 / Sizing confidence

Bring sizing into
the purchase decision.

Sizing confusion was a key source of friction. I brought sizing charts, visual aids, how-to guidance, and video into the PDP to address uncertainty before purchase.

What changed in the experience

The goal was to make fit and use part of product education. The project notes report fewer returns and fewer sizing-related support tickets after the new experience launched; no numeric reduction is available.

Decision 03 / Funnel consistency

The same promise,
from email to PDP.

The redesign extended across campaign pages and emails. Shared visual language and product storytelling helped the journey feel continuous when customers moved between touchpoints.

Cross-functional coordination

I coordinated design requests with marketing and engineering while the product experience was being rebuilt. Email campaigns also introduced the refreshed brand and drove traffic at launch, so the reported traffic lift reflects the wider launch effort.

04 / Design meets delivery

Two executions.
One launch.

The layered parallax interaction introduced engineering complexity. After several handoff iterations, we chose to ship two versions simultaneously: the parallax experience and a version using embedded video.

That choice kept the project moving and created a way to learn from real behavior. Both versions retained the same product narrative and visual direction.

These are static design views. The project notes document an A/B launch, but do not identify a winning variant or provide variant-level results.

A stronger experience.

CUSTOMER EXPERIENCE

Returns and sizing-related support tickets were reported to decrease after the PDP sizing improvements.

DELIVERY

Two implementations shipped within the final 4–6-week design, QA, and launch window.

FOUNDATION

A shared design direction connected the website, landing pages, and lifecycle touchpoints.

06 / What I took forward

The scroll caught attention.
The work underneath made it useful.

Looking outside the category helped the team develop a shared standard for the experience. Testing the reference early made that direction easier to discuss and carry into execution.

The project also sharpened my judgment around delivery. Shipping two considered implementations let us move forward while preserving the work that mattered: sizing clarity, product education, and continuity across the journey.

05 / Dr. Ruscio Institute · Healthcare UX

Care starts
with trust.

Helping patients find the right clinician before they book.

01 / The brief

Finding a clinician
shouldn’t require a long scroll.

The original Meet the Team page required visitors to scroll through substantial content before reaching Scott and Jake’s biographies. The brief asked for all three clinicians to appear at the top, with photos and names linking to their information.

It also requested an updated biography for Jake and a revised order for the detailed profiles. This was an information architecture problem as much as a visual refresh: the page needed to help visitors understand who was on the team and choose where to explore.

How might we help patients find a relevant clinician before asking them to read every biography?

02 / Information architecture

Overview first.
Depth by choice.

Meet

See the clinicians together at the start of the page.

Orient

Understand names, roles, and relevant affiliations.

Explore

Move into the profile that interests you.

Evaluate

Read biography, expertise, credentials, and research.

My working notes separated the clinical team from affiliations, questioned the order of information within the biographies, and identified a need for a stronger closing action. Those decisions shaped a page that can be scanned first and explored in greater detail afterward.

03 / Designing for trust

Make the people visible.
Make the evidence findable.

PEOPLE BEFORE PARAGRAPHS

A shared portrait row gives each clinician a visible place in the first scan of the page.

CREDIBILITY WITH A UX/UI Designer · contract

Affiliations occupy their own section instead of competing with the team introduction.

A CLEARER ROUTE INTO DETAIL

Names, roles, and a structured profile make it easier to choose where to read next.

Decision 01 / Profile structure

Turn a biography into
information people can navigate.

The exploration separates biography, areas of focus, research, publications, and professional background. This makes the content easier to maintain and gives visitors clearer cues about what each section can answer.

Why organize the content?

A long biography can contain useful information without making it easy to retrieve. The design work makes the categories visible and explores tabbed access so the visitor can choose a topic instead of scanning an undifferentiated block of text.

Decision 02 / Responsive hierarchy

The same clarity,
with less room.

Desktop and mobile designs preserve the team introduction and the separation between overview and detail. On a narrow screen, clinicians stack rather than relying on a compressed desktop row.

What I would validate next

I would check whether visitors can identify a relevant clinician, reach the correct profile, and explain the difference between expertise and credentials. I would also test the discoverability and keyboard behavior of the profile navigation in implementation.

04 / Design outcome

A more approachable team.
A clearer path into the details.

The design replaces a biography-first introduction with a team overview, distinct credibility content, and a more structured profile system. It responds directly to the brief’s concern about the effort required to reach the later clinicians.

The available material supports the design decisions and responsive layouts. It does not contain post-launch booking, engagement, or usability results, so this case study does not claim a measured increase in patient conversion.

05 / Reflection

Trust is easier to evaluate when the page makes its information easy to find.

The central design move was to separate the first impression from the deep read. A team overview provides orientation; structured profiles give the visitor somewhere useful to go next.

The next stage would be validating that navigation with patients and carrying the same care into implementation: anchor behavior, focus management, readable content, and a clear route toward booking.

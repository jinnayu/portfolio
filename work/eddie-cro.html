Overview

Instead of redesigning everything, we asked smaller questions.

Traffic was coming in, but not every landing page was turning that attention into action. Rather than rely on taste or a one-time overhaul, I designed a series of experiments around specific behavioral hypotheses.

The method

Design the experiment, not just the variant.

Each test began with a specific question around color, imagery, CTA treatment, messaging, or page context. That kept the work grounded in behavior instead of asking the less useful question: “Which version looks better?”

A winning visual isn’t a rule.
It’s evidence in context.

The interesting part

The same kind of change produced opposite winners.

Similar visual variables performed differently across two landing pages. Same product, similar design treatment — different user mindset. That pushed us to stop treating conversion patterns as universal design rules.

What worked depended on what the customer already knew, where they came from, and what decision the page was asking them to make.

Iteration

Every result became the brief for the next test.

A strong result gave us a pattern worth carrying forward. A weak result challenged an assumption. Conflicting outcomes forced us to understand the intent behind the click rather than simply copying the visual winner.

What I learned

Consistency is useful. Context is smarter.

The work changed how I approached future landing pages. I became less interested in best practices as fixed answers and more interested in turning them into testable starting points.
